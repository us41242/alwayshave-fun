Casino comps explained: what your play is worth

A comp is not a gift. It is a refund of a fixed slice of what the casino expects to win from you, computed from four numbers you control. Here is the formula the host runs on you, and a calculator that runs it on your own play.

On this page: theoretical loss · rated edge · the reinvestment rate · calculator · ADT and offers · the levers · what comps never do · sources

Theoretical loss: the only number the casino cares about

When a pit boss "rates" your play, or a slot club card tracks your coin-in, the casino is building one figure: your theoretical loss, called theo. It is not what you actually won or lost. It is what the math says you should have lost:

Theo = average bet × decisions per hour × hours × house edge
$25 blackjack, 70 hands an hour, 4 hours, rated at a 2% edge:
25 × 70 × 4 × 0.02 = $140 of theo.
Walk away $600 up or $600 down and the casino still values that session at $140.

This is why a lucky winning weekend still earns you an offer, and a brutal losing one does not earn extra. Actual results are noise to the casino. Theo is the signal, and everything they give you is priced from it.

Rated edge: the casino grades the game, not you

For table games the house edge in the formula is not your edge. It is a fixed number the property assigns to the game, based on how an average player plays it. Blackjack is commonly rated around 2%, even though a perfect basic-strategy player faces about 0.5% on a decent game. Craps pass line is rated near its true 1.4%. Double-zero roulette is 5.3%.

That gap matters. A basic-strategy blackjack player is being comped as if losing four times faster than they really are. Per dollar of real expected loss, blackjack played well is the best comp deal in the pit. Play badly and the gap closes to nothing.

Slots and video poker are the reverse: the machine reports exact coin-in, and the club pays points on it at a published rate. There is no rating floor to fight over, but there is also no way to be graded better than you play. A 9/6 Jacks or Better machine at 0.46% edge and a 10% penny slot earn the same points per dollar through the card. They cost you very different amounts to earn them.

The reinvestment rate: 30 to 40 cents on the theo dollar

Casinos give back a share of theo in comps, offers, and free play. The share is called the reinvestment rate. The widely reported range for Strip and locals properties is roughly 30 to 40 percent of theo, higher for top-tier hosted players and lower on games the property considers already generous. It is not published. It is inferred from what players report getting, and it moves with the property's mood and the calendar.

$140 theo × 35% ≈ $49 in comp value.
That is the buffet, or a chunk of a room rate, for four hours at $25 a hand.

Read that the other way round and you have the price list: every $1 of comp costs you about $2.50 to $3.30 of expected loss. A $300 "free" room is a $750 to $1,000 expected-loss purchase. That is the honest frame, and it is the one every credible comps writer uses.

ADT: why the offers come in the mail

Comps at the table are the small half. The big half is the mail: free rooms, free play, resort credit. Those are driven by average daily theo (ADT), your theo per gaming day averaged over recent trips, usually the last 12 months. A host or the marketing system sorts players into ADT bands and each band gets a tier of offers.

  • Fewer, bigger days beat many small ones. $600 of theo in one day is a $600 ADT. The same $600 spread over three days is a $200 ADT. Same money, worse offers.
  • A day counts as a day. Putting your card in for ten minutes on a day off from gambling drags the average down.
  • New cards get a look. Properties often send a new player their most aggressive offers to discover their real ADT. Once the system has a history it stops guessing in your favour.

The levers you actually control

  1. Play the low-edge version of whatever you play. Rated edge is per game, so perfect blackjack or a 9/6 video poker pay table earns the same points as the sloppy version at a fraction of the real cost.
  2. Slow down at the tables. Theo is per decision. A full table at 50 hands an hour racks up the same rating as an empty one at 200, and the pit tracks time and average bet, not hands. Fewer hands at the same rating is a smaller real loss.
  3. Get rated for every minute. Card in before the first bet. Unrated play is a donation.
  4. Ask, before you leave, not after. Comps are discretionary. The pit or host can only give what the theo supports, but they will not offer what you do not request.
  5. Concentrate play. One property, fewer days, bigger ADT. Splitting $500 of theo across three clubs makes you nobody's customer.
  6. Tournaments are theo you don't pay for. An invitational blackjack tournament is usually a comp itself. Your bet size in it is irrelevant to your rating, and the prize money is real. Learn the tournament math and practice it.

What comps never do

Comps lower the price of a trip you were going to take anyway. They do not turn a negative-expectation game positive. The numbers above make that plain: the casino returns a third of what it expects to win, and it expects to win. Anyone selling you a comp "system" that beats the house is selling the other two thirds.

The exceptions are narrow and well documented elsewhere: full-pay video poker with a strong enough cashback rate, and promotion stacking, where the total of points, mailers, and multipliers exceeds the edge. Both need a specific machine and a specific promotion and vanish the moment the casino notices. That is a separate subject and it is not the game most people are playing.
