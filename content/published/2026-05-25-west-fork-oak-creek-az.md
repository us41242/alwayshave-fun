There is a version of Sedona that most people don't find because they're busy staring at Cathedral Rock from the highway. It lives twelve miles north of town, tucked into a side canyon off Oak Creek, where the walls close in and the cottonwoods grow so dense they form a canopy over the water. The light comes through in pieces, falling in long columns through the leaves onto a creek the color of jade. West Fork Oak Creek is that version of Sedona. It's one of the most legitimately beautiful canyon walks in Arizona, and most days it feels nothing like the rest of the area's red-rock tourist circuit.

Today's score is 60/100 — Use Caution. I want to be honest with you about why, because the number is mostly about rain timing rather than a genuine hazard.

The creek crosses the path repeatedly throughout the 6.9-mile length, and while 24 cfs is easy to step across in dry conditions, a thunderstorm upstream changes that math fast. The notes on this trail exist for a reason: check water levels after rain.

If today is your only shot, go in the morning, watch the sky upstream to the northeast, and don't push all the way to the upper canyon if you see clouds building. The lower 3 miles are spectacular even as a shorter out-and-back.

## The AQI Question

AQI at 64 is Moderate, driven by ground-level ozone (O3), which is the standard Front Range and high desert signature for late May in sunny weather. Ozone builds through the day — it's a photochemical reaction that peaks in the afternoon. The practical fix is the same one I give for every moderate-ozone day: start before 9am and you're functionally ahead of the build. By Tuesday when the score jumps to 90, the AQI will likely follow the rain clearing.

Worth noting: N95 masks don't filter ozone. If you're in the sensitive-groups category (asthma, cardiovascular conditions, or hiking with kids who run at full output the entire time), morning timing is the move today. Out on the trail proper, you're in a shaded canyon with moving air and low midday exposure — it's more comfortable here than on the exposed red rock formations nearby.

## What the Hike Is

The trailhead is at the Call of the Canyon day-use area on AZ-89A — you'll need a Red Rock Pass ($5/day or $15/week) to park. The trail starts by crossing Oak Creek on a footbridge and immediately enters the West Fork canyon.

The first two miles are wide, clear, and easy. The canyon walls begin to narrow and the cottonwood canopy thickens. The creek crossings start in earnest around mile 1.5 — at 24 cfs they're step-overs or shallow wades, nothing technical. In May there may be lingering cold water from snowmelt upstream, so waterproof boots or trail runners you don't mind getting damp are worth considering.

The canyon gets progressively more dramatic as you go deeper. The walls climb toward 300 feet in the upper sections, the trail narrows, and the light — especially in the late afternoon — turns the red rock into something genuinely unrepeatable. This is why I drive from Flagstaff for this trail when I get the chance. The photography window at West Fork is the golden hour (around 7pm this time of year), when the low light hits the western wall and the cottonwood leaves pick up that warm backlit green. It doesn't last long. It is completely worth waiting for.

The full trail is 6.9 miles out and back with 400 feet of gain. The difficulty rating is Easy and that's accurate — there's no sustained climbing, just gradual elevation gain as the canyon lifts toward the head. Most hikers do 3–4 miles before turning around. The canyon feels worth the full length if you have time, especially in shoulder season before the summer crowds arrive.

## Kipper Logistics

She's coming. West Fork Oak Creek is dog-friendly (Kipper has been to this one before and is enthusiastic about it for reasons that are entirely creek-related). On-leash is the rule and I enforce it here — the canyon narrows significantly in the upper section and you don't want a loose dog scrambling up the talus slopes.

A few specifics:

- **Creek crossings** — she gets excited about these. Let her wade where the footing is good. The water is cold in May; it doesn't seem to bother her but she dries off quickly in the canyon air.
- **Water** — I carry her water separately because the creek water, while drinkable with a filter, isn't always accessible at the crossing points. She gets her own bowl at every break.
- **Surface** — the trail tread is dirt, gravel, and river rock. No slickrock, no technical sections. She handles this better than my ankles do honestly.

The Red Rock Pass parking situation means the lot can fill on weekends. If you're bringing dogs and kids both, arrive by 8am. The canyon rewards early arrivals with better light angles anyway.

## Olivia's Tip

Come Tuesday if you can. The forecast window of 90/100 from Tuesday through Thursday is as clean as this canyon gets in late May. The cottonwood leaves are in full green, the creek is running at a comfortable level, and the canyon crowds thin noticeably on weekdays. If you're driving from Phoenix or Tucson, Tuesday morning is the one.

And if you're only doing the first few miles: don't turn around before mile 2.5. That's where the walls close in and the light starts to do its thing. The first two miles are good. Everything past that is the reason people come back.
