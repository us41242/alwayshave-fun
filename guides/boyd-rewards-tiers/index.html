Boyd Rewards tiers: the one big Vegas program you can't price

Boyd Rewards has five tiers — Ruby at 0 Tier Credits, Sapphire at 750, Emerald at 15,000, Onyx at 50,000 and Titanium at 200,000 — earned January to December and kept for the rest of that year and all of the next. What Boyd does not publish is how fast any game earns a Tier Credit. Not slots, not video poker, not tables: all three read “based on game played, denomination, and time played.” So unlike Station, Caesars, Venetian and Resorts World, a Boyd tier cannot be costed from the outside. There is exactly one price Boyd puts in writing, and it is in a phone game: $4.99 of Stardust Social Casino coin purchases = 1 Tier Credit.

There is a sixth thing that looks like a tier and isn't. Onyx+ is a recognition programme, not a level: Boyd's FAQ says members who reach Onyx and earn 100,000 to 199,999 Tier Credits in the earning year may qualify for Onyx+ benefits the following year. The same 100,000 line shows up once in the benefit list, on reserved garage parking, which is offered only to members above 100,000 Tier Credits. Everything else about Onyx+ is unpublished.

Tier Credits come from slots, video poker, table games and bingo, are reset to zero every January 1, and have no cash value — they cannot be cashed out or taken as FastPlay. Earning is on a calendar year, so a tier reached in December is held for thirteen months and one reached in January for twenty-four.

The only published price, and it's a bad one

Every other big Vegas program names a rate you can multiply: Station's 50 status credits per hotel dollar, Caesars' 1 Tier Credit per $5 of reel coin-in, Resorts World's 5 per $1 of dining. Boyd names none for play. But its own points page prices Tier Credits in a different currency altogether — in-app purchases at Stardust Social Casino, where $4.99 of virtual coin purchases earns 1 Tier Credit and $0.99 earns 150 Boyd Points. That is a published, multipliable number, so here is every tier at it.

Nobody should do this, and that is the point of printing it. A number this large is still useful: it is the ceiling. Whatever a Boyd tier is worth to you, it is worth less than the only price Boyd itself will quote, and the gap between $3,742.50 and what a Sapphire card actually hands you — the right to start earning a 0.10% rebate — is the whole argument against chasing status you can't cost.

The 150-points-per-$0.99 row is the same story at a smaller scale. 150 Boyd Points are worth 15¢, so a dollar of Boyd Points bought this way costs $6.60, a return of about 15% on money spent. Boyd does not say whether the two rows stack on the same purchase, so the honest reading is that each is a separate published award and neither is a plan.

What Boyd Points are actually worth

Points are the spendable currency and they are priced plainly: 1,000 Boyd Points = $1.00, for FastPlay or at retail, and Boyd's points page adds that points earned on any game — slots, tables or video poker — can go to food or be downloaded to a slot machine. The earn side is published for machines and not for electronic tables.

Note that keno is grouped with reels, at the better rate, and that video poker earns at half the reel rate — the usual arrangement, and the usual trap, because the rebate is a rate on what you wager, not on what the game costs you. Boyd's three downtown casinos — the California, the Fremont and Main Street Station — sit in the Nevada Gaming Control Board's Downtown Las Vegas reporting area, where slot machines held 8.36% of the money put through them over the twelve months to 31 August 2026. Reading the rebate against that is the only honest way to read it.

$1 of Boyd Points is 1,000 points. On reels that is $1,000 of coin-in, which at an 8.36% hold is about $83.60 of expected loss. On 9/6 Jacks or Better it is $2,000 of coin-in — twice as much, because video poker earns half as fast — which at a 0.46% edge with perfect play is about $9.20. Same dollar of food. Nine times the price on the reels.

Points die quickly: six months of card inactivity forfeits them, per both the FAQ and rule 15 of the programme rules. FastPlay redemptions need a $1 minimum, which is 1,000 points. The video poker trainer teaches the 9/6 Jacks or Better strategy the $9.20 figure assumes; without it the edge is worse and so is the comparison.

The Ruby trap

Boyd's rules say a member “may receive Boyd Points based on tier level”, and the points page spells out what that means: you start earning Boyd Points once you reach Sapphire. Below 750 Tier Credits the card tracks your play, builds your Tier Credit balance and your offers, and pays you nothing spendable.

So the first 750 Tier Credits are the most valuable on the ladder — they turn the rebate from 0% to 0.10% — and they are also the ones nobody can price for you, because Boyd publishes no credit rate for any game. The practical consequence is simple and worth more than any tier chart: a Boyd card is worth the least on your first trip, and the only way to shorten the dead zone is to be carded from the first hand of the first day, including at tables and bingo, where credits are awarded on a host's observation rather than automatically. Ask at a Boyd Rewards desk where your balance stands; it is the one number that decides whether your play is being paid for.

Two readings worth having. First, Sapphire is the only threshold on this chart that changes the economics: it is where points and carryover begin. Emerald adds the travel benefits and the two VIP rows; Onyx and Titanium mostly swap one annual gift for another, which is why Titanium shows “no” on the Onyx luxury-gift row — Boyd lists them as different awards, not as a stack. Second, there is no dining comp anywhere on this chart at any tier, exactly as at Station. Food at Boyd is bought with points, and points are earned at the same published rate at every tier from Sapphire up. Status does not change the rate.

Beyond this chart Boyd lists roughly eighty location-specific benefits — birthday comps, waived check-cashing fees, slot-machine holds of one to two hours, spa and banquet discounts of 10% to 20%, cabanas, show seating, a personal host at the top — with no tier mapping published centrally and the standing caveat that they vary by property. Nobody outside Boyd can tell you which of those your card gets; the Boyd Rewards desk at your property can.

Year-end carryover

Your balance is zeroed every January 1, but Sapphire and above carry over whatever sits above their band's floor, capped. Boyd's own chart and its own two examples:

Year-end Tier CreditsCarryover
750 – 14,999Up to 500
15,000 – 49,000Up to 5,000
50,000 – 199,999Up to 10,000
200,000+Up to 40,000

Boyd's examples: a year-end balance of 18,000 carries 3,000 (18,000 − 15,000); a balance of 280,000 carries 40,000, the cap. Credits are awarded no later than January 30, only on Boyd property-earned credits, and only above a 750 minimum. One wrinkle to know about rather than trust: the carryover chart's second band reads 15,000 – 49,000 while the tier chart on Boyd's points page reads 15,000 – 49,999. A balance between 49,001 and 49,999 falls in a gap the two charts disagree about; ask rather than assume.

Boyd or MGM?

A question people actually search, and it has a clean answer that isn't about which perks look nicer. Both programmes refuse to publish a per-dollar Tier Credit rate for gambling. The difference is what else they publish.

So each programme publishes the half the other hides. If you want to know what your play pays back, Boyd tells you and MGM does not. If you want to know what status costs, MGM tells you — Gold is 75,000 Tier Credits, which is $18,750 of hotel, dining and spa at 4 per $1 — and Boyd does not, at any price short of the social casino. Neither publishes enough to plan both at once. Every Vegas programme's base rate, side by side, is in players cards compared, and how comps are actually calculated is the guide behind all of it.
