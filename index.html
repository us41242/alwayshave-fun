The last hand of a blackjack tournament is arithmetic

In a tournament you are not playing the house, you are playing the other stacks, and the table is decided by arithmetic on the last hand or two. The usual mistake is to copy the leader. Say the leader has $2,600 and bets $500, you have $1,800, and you bet after them. Here is what two bets do across the four ways the hand can land (even-money results, no doubles):

Hand resultYou copy: bet $500You chase: bet $1,325
You win, leader loses$2,300 vs $2,100 — win$3,125 vs $2,100 — win
Both win$2,300 vs $3,100 — lose$3,125 vs $3,100 — win
Both lose$1,300 vs $2,100 — lose$475 vs $2,100 — lose
You lose, leader wins$1,300 vs $3,100 — lose$475 vs $3,100 — lose

The chase bet is gap + leader’s bet + one chip: $800 + $500 + $25 = $1,325. It wins both results where you win the hand; the copy wins only one. The both-lose row is the one you give up, and the dealer decides the rest. Flip it around and the math flips: if the leader bets big and you cannot reach them, bet the minimum and let the dealer beat you both. A player loses about 49% of blackjack hands (wizardofodds.com), so “lose with the leader” is often your best line. And if you are the leader, match the biggest threat’s bet, so only the split result can beat you.

Knowing this at a desk is easy. Doing it with a dealer waiting and five stacks you had to count yourself is the whole tournament. Final Hand drills it with a coach; the bet sizer does the sum for any three numbers; the strategy guide covers doubles, betting to lose and the held-back chip, from Stanford Wong and Kenneth Smith.

Is a tournament worth the entry fee?

Before the last hand comes a simpler question: is the tournament worth entering at all? If the entrants play about equally well, each seat’s expected share of the prize pool is the pool divided by the number of entrants, however the prizes are split. So the math is one division. A $10,000 guaranteed pool with 40 entrants is worth $250 a seat. At a $200 entry, an average player is $50 ahead before the first hand. At 60 entrants the seat is worth $167, and the same player is $33 behind. The break-even field is pool ÷ entry fee, which is 50 entrants here.

That is why the word to look for on a tournament flyer is guaranteed. The casino owes that pool whether 30 people show up or 300, so a small field can make the entry a good bet for an average player. When the pool is only the entry fees paid back, the average seat is worth exactly the fee. Any cut the casino keeps is an edge you have to make up with skill. A room, meals or free play that come with the entry count on your side.

“Skill you need” is how many times an average entrant’s share you would have to win, on average, to get your fee back. The average share is exact for a field of equal players, wherever the prizes fall. Rebuys, add-ons and taxes on big prizes are left out. Skill is where Final Hand comes in: most tournament tables are decided by the bet on the last hand or two, and that bet is learnable.

What a casino tier really costs you

A players-club tier has two prices, and the programs publish only one. The published price is coin-in — how much money has to pass through the machine. The price you actually pay is expected loss, and that depends entirely on which game you run the coin-in through. Only four Vegas programs publish a per-dollar Tier Credit rate for machine play — Caesars, Station, Venetian and Resorts World — so only those four can be priced from outside, and the tier price calculator prices every tier they publish. Here is Caesars Diamond, the tier most people chase, priced four ways.

Caesars pays 1 Tier Credit per $5 on reel slots and 1 per $10 on video poker, so video poker needs twice the coin-in for the same status — and still costs about a ninth as much, because the edge it charges you is seventeen times smaller. The one-day rows use Caesars' own daily Tier Credit bonus: earn 5,000 credits inside one promotional day and it adds 10,000, which is Diamond that day. Nothing changes but the scheduling.

And the bonus is not a one-time trick. Caesars pays it “every day”, one level per promotional day, so it repeats across days, and that matters most at the top. Diamond Elite is 75,000 Tier Credits: spread across the year on reel slots that is $375,000 of coin-in and about $30,000 of expected loss. Five separate promotional days of 5,000 earned credits each is 25,000 earned plus 50,000 in bonuses, exactly 75,000, for $125,000 of coin-in and about $10,000. Run those same five days through 9/6 video poker played correctly and it is $250,000 of coin-in and about $1,150. Same card at one twenty-sixth of the price, and it is five trips’ worth of the time catch below, not one.

Two catches the table cannot show. Time: $50,000 through a machine in one day is about 67 hours at $1.25 a hand and a bit over three hours at $25 a hand, so the cheap one-day route is a high-denomination play, not a quarter one. Skill: the 0.46% only exists if you play the hand right, and that is what the video poker trainer drills. Play video poker like a slot machine and you are back in the $6,000 column.

Even at $230 this is money spent on status, not a way to come out ahead: Diamond waives resort fees and feeds you once a year, so price that against whichever column applies to you before you chase it.
