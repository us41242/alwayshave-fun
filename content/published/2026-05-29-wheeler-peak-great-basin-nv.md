## Wheeler Peak, Great Basin NP — Score 60/100

**Score: 60/100 — Use Caution**

24°F at the summit zone. Feels like 16°F with a 12 mph wind. AQI 49 — Good. Rain probability at 22%. Wheeler Peak Scenic Drive closed until late June.

I want to talk about Wheeler Peak today because I have been surprised how few people outside of Nevada know this summit exists, and more people should be planning it. At 13,063 feet, it is the second-highest peak in Nevada, capped by a remnant ice field — one of only a handful at this latitude in the continental United States — and the hiking is exactly the kind of big-day alpine objective that Front Range Colorado hikers will recognize immediately. The difference is that you will probably have the summit to yourself.

The 60/100 score reflects two converging realities: the temperature (24°F at summit elevation, feels like 16°F), and the road. The Wheeler Peak Scenic Drive — the road that gets you to the upper trailhead at 9,886 feet — does not open until late June. If you drive to Great Basin NP today and try to reach the standard trailhead, you will hit a gate partway up. The full summit from the upper trailhead is the 8.6-mile, 2,900-foot objective. Right now, that upper trailhead does not exist for you.

This is not a wasted trip article. But it is an eyes-open one.

## What Wheeler Peak Actually Is

I want to get specific about this because the Nevada mountain reputation does not do Wheeler Peak justice.

The Great Basin NP version of Wheeler Peak — not to be confused with the Wheeler Peak in the Sangre de Cristo range in New Mexico — sits in the Snake Range in White Pine County, Nevada. It is 65 miles east of Ely, a town that even most Nevada residents could not place without a map. The isolation is part of the deal. There is no cell service for the last 40-plus miles of the approach. The nearest chain grocery is an hour away. The park sees fewer visitors in an entire year than Rocky Mountain NP sees in a week.

What the isolation gives you: a summit at 13,063 feet where you may genuinely be alone, or close to it, in July. The glacial cirque below the summit contains Wheeler Peak Glacier — a remnant ice body that has held on at this latitude because of the mountain's aspect and elevation, and which is one of the last relict glaciers in the Great Basin. The blue ice and the moraine below it look like something transplanted from a Cascade or Sierra peak.

The trail from the upper trailhead (9,886 ft) to the summit gains 2,900 feet over 4.3 miles one way. That gain rate — 675 feet per mile — is comparable to a hard Front Range day. Unlike the Colorado 14ers, which have developed trail infrastructure and predictable crowd logistics, Wheeler Peak's upper trail is maintained but does not have the same polished quality. You are on it more as a solo proposition.

The summit: 13,063 feet with views of the Snake Range to the south and a textbook Basin-and-Range grid of valleys in every direction. On a clear day, you can see mountain ranges 150 miles away. The Great Basin is big and empty and Wheeler Peak sits high enough to show you how big.

## The Road Situation — What You Can Actually Do Right Now

Wheeler Peak Scenic Drive opens late June, typically around June 20–25 depending on snowpack. Check the NPS website at nps.gov/grba for the current status — they update it when the road opens. Once it opens, the drive to the upper trailhead at Wheeler Peak Campground (10,161 ft) takes about 12 miles from the park entrance.

What you can do today:

- **Lehman Caves** — one of the better cave systems in the national park system, maintained at 50°F year-round, guided tours running from the visitor center. Worth a few hours on any visit regardless of summit plans
- **Alpine Lakes Loop** — a shorter accessible route from the Lehman Caves area that gets you into the spruce-fir transition zone without the summit elevation
- **Bristlecone Pine Trail** — a loop to the ancient bristlecone grove (trees exceeding 4,000 years old), accessible from a lower trailhead; check the current road status for which portions are open
- **Lexington Arch Trail** — in the southern part of the park, a 3.4-mile round trip to a natural limestone arch at 7,200 feet, accessible well before the scenic drive opens

If you are planning your summer calendar and Wheeler Peak is on the list: late July through mid-August is the sweet spot. The road is fully open, snow is gone from all but the glacier itself, thunderstorm risk is lower before noon, and you are hiking through wildflower meadows below treeline before hitting the talus above.

## Altitude and the Colorado Comparison

For anyone who hikes Colorado regularly and has been looking for an equivalent experience without the 14er crowds: this is it.

Wheeler Peak at 13,063 feet requires exactly the same altitude respect as a hard Colorado hike. Coming from sea level and jumping straight to the upper trailhead at nearly 10,000 feet without acclimatization is asking for headaches and nausea. The standard prescription: spend a night at Great Basin before attempting the summit. Wheeler Peak Campground sits above 10,000 feet — sleeping there the night before is meaningful acclimatization for anyone not coming from high altitude already.

Symptoms to take seriously on the ascent: headache, nausea, dizziness, unusual fatigue, loss of appetite. These are altitude responses, not "I'm tired from hiking." The fix is to descend. The summit will be there the following morning. There is no shame in turning around at 12,000 feet if your body is telling you something useful.

Start before 7am when the road opens in season. Thunderstorm risk in the Snake Range follows the same afternoon pattern as the San Juans and Sawatch in Colorado — clear mornings, convection building after noon, lightning risk on exposed summit terrain by 1–2pm. Be off the summit ridgeline by noon. This is not a suggestion that is optional at 13,000 feet.

Two layers of shells in the summit pack regardless of the valley forecast. The temperature differential between the park entrance and the summit is 30–40 degrees on a warm day. The wind at the summit ridgeline is not the wind you drove through on the way in.

## Mark and Hank

The trail is dog-friendly, which Wheeler Peak explicitly is within the national park boundary. Mark and Hank are both invited when the road opens.

A few specifics for the summer version of this trip:

- **Altitude for small dogs** — shitzus at 12–14 pounds are working harder per step at high elevation than a larger dog. Watch for excessive panting, disorientation, or refusal to continue; altitude responses in dogs present similarly to humans
- **Temperature** — even on a 75°F valley day, the exposed summit ridge will likely be in the 40s. They need insulation for the sections above treeline, and I bring a small fleece for each of them on cold-summit days
- **Talus** — the upper mile of the summit route is talus field. Mark and Hank have navigated technical terrain before, but loose angular rock above 12,500 feet is where I watch them most carefully and let them set the pace
- **Water** — carry water for them. The upper sections above the glacier have limited reliable water sources, and waiting for them to drink at every trickle adds time you want for the summit window

Kelly and I are tentatively planning this for late July when the road opens and the wildflower timing is right. Mark and Hank's opinion of their own high-altitude capability remains characteristically overconfident. We will mediate that at the trailhead.

## The Fire Situation

Low risk. Nearest reported fire at 129.6 km, none within 50 km. The Great Basin NP sits in a fire shadow relative to much of Nevada — the Snake Range topography and the park's location on the Nevada-Utah border keeps it relatively clean on most seasons. AQI 49 is Good, O3-driven, not smoke. This is one of the cleanest air readings in the Great Basin right now.

For a summer summit day when the road is open: check conditions the morning of. Fire behavior in Nevada can shift quickly in dry wind events, and the AQI picture at the park entrance does not always match the summit zone. The conditions page updates every 30 minutes.

## John's Take

Wheeler Peak is one of the great underrated alpine objectives in the American West. The isolation is a feature, not a bug. The summit glacier is worth the drive on its own. And the views from 13,063 feet across the Great Basin are the kind that recalibrate your sense of scale in a way that Front Range peaks, surrounded by other 14ers, do not quite do.

Come in July. Check the road status before you make the drive — it is 5 hours from Las Vegas, 6 from Salt Lake City, and you do not want to make that trip to find the scenic drive still gated. Spend the night at Wheeler Peak Campground. Start before 7am. Bring shells regardless of the valley forecast. Be off the summit by noon.

The 60/100 score today is honest about the current access situation. The mountain this summer, when the road opens and the conditions align, is a very different story.
