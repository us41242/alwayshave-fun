## What the Wind Means Here

19 mph sustained with gusts to 31. On most trails this is a wind-layer recommendation, maybe a hat situation.

On Angels Landing it's a different conversation.

The chain section — the final 0.4 miles to the summit — follows a narrow sandstone spine. On both sides there's a drop of several hundred feet. The chains exist because the terrain requires them, not as a grab-rail for nervous hikers. At 31 mph gusts, you're holding the chains because the physics of the situation require it.

This is not a "turn around" recommendation. People hike Angels Landing in these conditions regularly. But it's a "grip the chain with both hands on the exposed sections and wait for gusts to pass before moving" situation, which is worth knowing going in rather than discovering at the first exposed section when you weren't expecting it.

The canyon funnels wind, and wind on that ridgeline feels stronger than the weather station reading at the valley floor. If you're going to the summit today, go with that understanding, not with the expectation of an easy afternoon stroll.

## Late May at Angels Landing

The calendar is in your favor right now. April through June is one of Angels Landing's two optimal windows (the other is September–October). The summer heat that makes canyon hiking a heat-management exercise hasn't set in yet. Wildflowers are showing along the canyon walls on the approach from the trailhead. The falls at the canyon bottom — visible from the road as you drive in — are still running from snowmelt.

This is peak season for Zion Valley in terms of visitors, which means the shuttle is running full schedules and the trailhead is active. That's a logistics reality, not a reason to skip the trip.

## The Permit System — What Actually Happens

Angels Landing requires a permit for the chain section, available through Recreation.gov. There are two ways to get one:

**Seasonal lottery:** Applications open in January for the full season. If you haven't applied, this window is closed.

**Day-before and day-of lottery:** A smaller pool of permits releases the evening before and morning of the hike date. These are real opportunities — competitive, but not impossible. The day-of pool releases at 6am mountain time. Have the Recreation.gov app open and ready before 6.

Day-use permits cost $3 per person. The permit covers the chain section access; the rest of the trail (to Scout Lookout) does not require a permit.

One note on this: Scout Lookout, the flat area at the base of the chain section, is a legitimate destination on its own. The views from Scout Lookout look straight down Zion Canyon with Angels Landing's summit above you. If you don't get a permit, it's not a wasted trip.

## What the Hike Is

The trail starts at the Grotto Trailhead, accessible by shuttle from the Zion Canyon Visitor Center (private vehicles are restricted from Zion Canyon Scenic Drive — you're taking the shuttle regardless). The trail follows the canyon floor across the river, then climbs.

The first section up to Scout Lookout involves Refrigerator Canyon, a shaded narrow stretch that stays cool on warm days, followed by Walter's Wiggles — a series of 21 tight switchbacks cut directly into the cliff face. They're steep, they're relentless, and they're exactly as much work as they look like on the trail map. By the top of the Wiggles you've done most of the elevation gain.

Scout Lookout at 4,290 feet is the decision point: go up the chains or turn around. The chain section from Scout Lookout to the summit is 0.4 miles and gains an additional 200 feet on terrain ranging from wide sandstone paths to narrow spine with 1,000+ foot drops on both sides.

In today's wind conditions, take your time on the exposed sections. The chains are fixed for a reason. There is no shame in moving slowly through the exposed parts.

## The AQI Situation

AQI at 61 is Moderate, driven by ozone. The Zion Canyon is a deep sandstone canyon with good air movement from the Virgin River corridor, and the ozone exposure in the canyon itself tends to be lower than the mesa-top AQI readings. Still: start early if you're in the sensitive-group category. The trail is physical enough that you'll be working at elevated heart rates — that's when AQI matters most for anyone with respiratory or cardiovascular considerations.

Morning timing is the right call for reasons beyond AQI. The shuttle lines are shorter, the canyon is cooler, and the light in Walter's Wiggles is better before noon anyway.

---

## No Dogs

Mark and Hank are not going. Zion National Park does not allow dogs on any park trails — no exceptions, no designated hiking paths where they're permitted. The developed areas and the Pa'rus Trail (paved, flat, follows the river) allow leashed dogs, but Angels Landing is emphatically not that trail.

If you're traveling with dogs and you want them on the hike, look at the BLM land surrounding Zion or the Hurricane Cliffs area to the west. Both have good off-park options that are dog-accessible. The park itself is not the answer.

## John's Take

78/100 at Angels Landing in late May means the conditions are legitimate. The wind is the one variable that requires active attention, not because it makes the hike impossible, but because the chain section demands that you not be casual about it. Grip the chains. Wait for gusts. Don't rush the exposed sections.

Everything else is working in your favor: temperature, minimal rain, prime season calendar, full trail access. Get the day-of permit if you don't have one — the 6am Recreation.gov release is worth setting an alarm for. Shoot for a 7am shuttle departure from the visitor center. Be off the summit before 11am when the heat and crowds both peak.

The summit view down Zion Canyon from 5,790 feet on a clear morning is worth the wind and the Wiggles and the permit lottery. It's the view that made Zion famous. It holds up.
