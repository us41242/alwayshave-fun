There is a part of the Grand Canyon system that most people don't know exists — not because it's hidden, exactly, but because getting there requires advance planning that most hikers don't do in time. Eight miles in from the trailhead at Hualapai Hilltop, at the bottom of a canyon the Havasupai people have called home for centuries, a series of waterfalls drop into travertine pools so intensely blue-green they look like they've been edited. The kind of blue that makes you stop and double-check your own eyes.

Havasu Falls is that place. It scores 70/100 today — Good conditions — and late May is a real window if you already have permits in hand.

If you don't have permits yet, keep reading, because the permit situation here is the first thing to understand before anything else.

## The Permit Situation — This Is the Real Planning Challenge

I am going to be direct with you: permits for Havasu Falls sell out months in advance. Usually within minutes of the permit window opening each February for the coming season. If you don't have a permit in hand right now, you are not going this week. This is not a trail you can spontaneously decide to hike.

The permits are managed by the Havasupai Tribe through their official tourism site at thehavasupaitribe.com. The cost structure includes a per-person entry fee plus a camping fee (Havasu Falls requires an overnight — there is no day-use permit for the falls themselves). Camping at Havasupai Campground, located about a mile past the falls, is how virtually all visitors experience this place.

Here's what I tell people when they've missed the February window: watch for cancellation releases, which happen throughout the season. Set up alerts on the tribal tourism site. Be ready to book immediately when a slot opens. It's not a guarantee, but cancellations do happen.

The permit logistics are genuinely part of the experience here. Everyone who makes it to those pools has gone through the same planning gauntlet. There's a certain shared understanding among the hikers you'll meet at Havasupai.

## What This Hike Actually Is

Havasu Falls is 20 miles round-trip with 2,800 feet of total elevation change. It's rated Moderate, which is accurate if you adjust for desert distance and heat.

The trail starts at Hualapai Hilltop, accessible via a 63-mile dirt road from Route 66. The first section descends steeply into Hualapai Canyon before joining Havasu Creek in the canyon bottom. From there, it's 8 miles of flat-to-gentle trail along the creek to the village of Supai — the only U.S. village that still receives mail by pack mule — and then another mile or so to the campground.

The falls themselves are scattered across the last few miles:

- **Fifty Foot Falls** — a smaller cascade about a mile before the main falls, often passed quickly on the way in
- **Havasu Falls** — the main event, 100 feet of turquoise water hitting a travertine pool big enough to swim in. The color comes from calcium carbonate in the creek. It's not a trick of the light; it genuinely looks that way in all weather
- **Mooney Falls** — a 200-foot drop accessed via a heart-rate-elevating series of chains and carved tunnels in the cliff face, downstream from camp. The most dramatic of the set and worth the effort if your hands are steady

Most people stay two to three nights to do the full canyon justice. The falls are the destination but the canyon itself — the red walls, the cottonwood shade, the sound of water in desert silence — is what people remember long after the photos fade.

## Late May Specifically

Late May is a shoulder-season window at Havasu. The summer heat is arriving but hasn't peaked — July and August regularly see temperatures above 100°F in the canyon floor, which changes the trip significantly. Right now at 83°F, you're in a zone where starting early and carrying adequate water makes the distance manageable.

The travertine pools are at their most vibrant in late spring before summer algae growth affects visibility. Flow rates from snowmelt have typically stabilized by late May, meaning the falls are running full without the flash flood risk that comes with spring storms earlier in the season.

The campground is occupied but not at peak summer capacity. Late May weekdays are the sweet spot if your permit has flexibility.

## The AQI Question

AQI at 87 is Moderate, O3-driven. In the canyon bottom at Havasu, you're typically 3,000+ feet below the rim and surrounded by canyon walls with moving air. Ozone exposure at the canyon floor tends to be lower than at the rim for most of the day, simply because of the elevation differential and canyon air dynamics.

That said: if you're hiking with kids, or if you have asthma or cardiovascular sensitivities, an 8am departure from the trailhead addresses the exposure window before ozone builds to its afternoon peak. Afternoon canyon hiking in late May is warm anyway — you want to be at camp well before 2pm regardless.

## Logistics

**Trailhead:** Hualapai Hilltop Trailhead — drive I-40 to Route 66 near Peach Springs, AZ, then 63 miles on Indian Route 18, which is paved but narrow in places. The last stretch can be rough after rain. Standard vehicles are fine in dry conditions.

**Water:** Havasu Creek is your lifeline. The trail passes directly along the creek for most of the distance into camp, so water sources are available. Still, carry 3–4 liters from the trailhead for the initial descent — the first miles before you reach the creek are exposed.

**Timing:** Start no later than 6am from the Hualapai Hilltop. The first section descends into the canyon before the heat builds. By the time you hit the canyon bottom and the shade of the cottonwoods, you'll have earned it. Arriving at camp before noon gives you the afternoon at the falls in the best light.

**What to bring:** 3 liters minimum for the approach. Electrolytes — heat and wind together deplete sodium faster than most people expect. A dry bag for your phone (waterfall spray at the pools is real). Camp shoes for the creek crossings. Trekking poles are optional but the descent into the canyon is knee-intensive and worth the support on the return.

## Olivia's Tip

Book a three-night trip if you can. Two nights technically works, but three means a day with no agenda except the falls and the canyon. That's the version where you find the upper pools before sunrise, sit at the edge of Mooney Falls with coffee, and actually absorb where you are.

The light at Havasu Falls around 4pm in late May is extraordinary — the canyon walls are in full sun and the water picks up the golden color from the travertine. Bring a camera with a fast shutter speed for the waterfall spray and accept that your phone photos won't capture the color. They never do. The eyes are better here.

Plan your permit. Do the trip. It lives up.
